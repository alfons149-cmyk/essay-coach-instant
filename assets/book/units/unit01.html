Unit 1

Clauses, Phrases and Complete Sentences

Before we can write clear, elegant essays, we must first understand what English writing is built on — the sentence. Every sentence has a structure, and that structure depends on clauses and phrases. Think of them as the building blocks of meaning. Once you know how to use these blocks, you can build anything — from a short, simple sentence to a long, beautifully connected paragraph.

1. What is a clause?

A clause is a grammatical unit built around a verb. In the ordinary statement clauses we practise first, find the subject and the finite verb: Pupils compare costs. The subject is pupils; compare is the finite verb.

A finite verb carries tense or works with an auxiliary: compares, compared, can compare. Ask who or what the clause is about and what is said about it. In The cost of the books is high, the subject head is singular cost, so use is, not are.

This is a starting method, not a definition requiring an explicit subject in every clause. In an instruction such as Check your answer, the subject you is understood. Non-finite clauses such as to compare the prices also occur; learn the complete main-clause pattern first.

2. Independent and dependent clauses

An independent clause can form a complete sentence: The pupil checked the price. A dependent clause contributes meaning but cannot normally stand alone in a formal essay: because the price seemed high.

Because the price seemed high, the pupil checked it again. The opening gives a reason; the main clause completes the message. You can reverse them: The pupil checked it again because the price seemed high.

Words such as because, although, when and if introduce common dependent clauses. Relative clauses also depend on other material: the pupil who checked the price. Do not assume that every dependent clause begins with one of the same four words.

3. Phrases inside clauses

A phrase works as a unit inside a larger structure. A noun phrase can be a subject: the main benefit of these sessions. A preposition phrase can add place or time: in the library. Neither example forms an independent statement.

For the first exercises, distinguish a phrase such as after the lesson from a finite clause such as after the lesson ended. Do not rely only on length: a long noun phrase may still lack a main verb.

Terminology for non-finite constructions varies in teaching materials. Here, to improve an answer is treated as a non-finite clause; it cannot form a complete essay sentence by itself.

4. Complete essay sentences

A complete written sentence normally contains an independent clause, begins with a capital and ends with sentence punctuation. Although the task was difficult. has punctuation but still leaves its relationship incomplete.

Although the task was difficult, the pupil made a useful plan. The main clause makes a claim; the subordinate clause adds a concession. An imperative such as Make a plan is also complete, with an understood subject.

Check the finite verb as well as the subject: The pupil working quietly is not a complete statement; The pupil was working quietly is. A sentence may contain several related ideas, so a full stop is not simply a sign that you have paused.

5. Understanding Sentence Patterns

English usually follows this pattern:

Subject + Verb + Object + (Extra Information)

Example:

I ate my pizza quickly in the car last night because I was in a hurry.

  • Subject: I
  • Verb: ate
  • Object: my pizza
  • Manner: quickly
  • Location: in the car
  • Time: last night
  • Reason: because I was in a hurry

Good vs. unnatural word order:

  • Yesterday, I ate my pizza… ✔
  • I yesterday ate my pizza… ✘

6. Two useful starting patterns

Part 1 uses movement and non-movement examples to help you arrange information. These are practice patterns, not an exhaustive classification of English sentences.

Movement: I went to the city centre by bus this morning to buy a shirt. The destination sits close to went; the other phrases add means, time and purpose.

Verb and object: I ate my pizza quickly in the car last night. Keep ate my pizza together before adding optional detail.

Other patterns are equally important: She smiled (no object); The task is useful (subject complement); They gave pupils advice (two objects). Choose a pattern that fits the verb and intended meaning. You do not need every optional element in every sentence.

7. Types of Sentences

To write advanced Cambridge essays, you must use a variety of sentence types.

a) Simple Sentence

One independent clause.

  • I study English every day.
  • The exam starts at nine o’clock.

b) Compound Sentence

Two independent clauses joined by a connector.

  • I studied hard, and I passed the exam.
  • The weather was bad, but we still went for a walk.

c) Complex Sentence

One independent clause with at least one dependent clause.

Example: Because it was my birthday, we ate out at a restaurant.

d) Compound–Complex Sentence

At least two independent clauses + one dependent clause.

Example: Because I had a headache, I went home, and I took an aspirin.

8. Connecting your clauses

Coordinating conjunction: The task was difficult, but the pupil continued. Both clauses could stand alone.

Subordinating conjunction: Although the task was difficult, the pupil continued. The although-clause now depends on the main clause.

Linking adverb: The task was difficult. However, the pupil continued. A semicolon is another option: The task was difficult; however, the pupil continued. The adverb shows a relationship but does not license joining two main clauses with a comma alone.

Select the relationship before selecting the connector. A difficult task does not necessarily cause persistence, so replacing however with therefore would change the argument.

9. Putting It All Together

Example paragraph:

In England, there are speed limits clearly displayed on the roads to show the maximum speed you should drive. Some drivers just don’t seem to notice these, and they treat every trip as a race, overtaking in dangerous places like on a blind bend. The speed limits are there for a reason, and it’s always worrying to see people driving like this.

This paragraph uses a mix of simple, compound, and complex sentences, plus precise phrases to add detail.

10. Quick Practice

  1. Identify the clause type:
    a) When I was young, I loved reading fairy tales.
    b) I love reading fairy tales.
    c) Because I love fairy tales.
  2. Complete the sentence:
    Although it was raining… → Although it was raining, we decided to go out.
  3. Combine:
    I was tired. I finished my homework. → Although I was tired, I finished my homework.

11. Final Thoughts

You’ve just taken the first step toward mastering essay writing. Understanding clauses, phrases, and sentences is like learning how to build a house. If the foundations are strong, everything else — paragraphs, essays, and even exam tasks — becomes easier.

Clear sentences lead to clear ideas. And clear ideas lead to confident writing.
