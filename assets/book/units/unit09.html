1. Read the requirements before choosing a structure

B2 First Writing Part 1 asks for an essay on a given title, using two supplied ideas and an idea of your own. Write 140-190 words. Your own idea must be relevant and distinct enough to add something; changing the wording of a supplied point is not a new contribution.

The entire Writing paper lasts 80 minutes for two tasks. Around 40 minutes for this essay is a practice allocation, not a separately imposed timer. Follow the instructions on your actual paper.

The general opinion tasks in Part 2 of this course teach how to reason. Before using that process in B2, add the task-specific content check. You cannot ignore a supplied point because your preferred essay template has only two body paragraphs.

2. What to check in your answer

Content: have you covered the actual question and all required ideas? Communicative Achievement: does the essay suit its purpose and reader? Organisation: can the reader follow the development? Language: are vocabulary and structures appropriate and controlled?

These are dimensions for assessing the complete text. A difficult word, a long sentence or a particular connector does not earn a guaranteed score. Your position can be positive, negative or conditional if it answers the task and is supported.

3. Turn required content into paragraph jobs

Original practice task: Is making exams more difficult the best way to improve education? Notes: fairness; motivation; your own idea. Use the notes and give reasons for your view.

A useful plan might group motivation and meaningful difficulty together, then discuss fair assessment and feedback. Grouping is acceptable when both supplied ideas and the extra idea remain clear. A separate paragraph for every point is another possibility if you can develop each within the length.

An introduction establishes the issue and direction. Body paragraphs explain reasons using relevant detail. A conclusion draws the answer together. The task determines the structure; four or five paragraphs are not universal requirements.

4. Develop rather than list

A claim such as Hard exams motivate pupils needs an explanation. Ask: under what circumstances could challenge encourage preparation? What happens if a test covers material pupils have not been taught?

A concrete example illustrates a mechanism. It is not research evidence merely because it sounds plausible. Use could when describing an imagined classroom situation. Keep the ending within what the paragraph supports.

Your own point could be feedback after assessment. Explain how identifying an error and practising the missing skill could help. Do not just add the word feedback to a list.

5. Budget words for the whole task

For a practice target of 170 words, you could allocate about 25 to the introduction, 60 to the first body paragraph, 60 to the second and 25 to the conclusion. This is a planning aid, not a scoring formula.

Count words rather than lines of handwriting. If the draft is short, check for missing content or explanation. If it is long, remove repeated framing and irrelevant detail before cutting necessary development. Do not assume that an examiner simply stops reading at a fixed word beyond the limit.

6. Read a complete model after your own attempt

Original teaching model: 156 words. This is not an officially graded script.

Exams can show what pupils understand, but making them harder is not necessarily the best way to improve education. Schools should consider fairness, motivation and the support pupils receive.

More demanding questions may encourage pupils to prepare carefully. However, difficulty is useful only when questions test the knowledge and skills taught in class. Otherwise, pupils may feel that success depends on guessing what will appear. Clear expectations could therefore support motivation more effectively than simply raising the difficulty.

Fairness also matters. A single difficult written test may not show what a pupil can explain in a practical task. Using suitable assessment methods would give teachers a fuller picture. In addition, feedback after a test could help pupils identify an error and practise the missing skill before the next assessment.

Overall, schools should focus on meaningful assessment and useful feedback. Harder exams may have a place, but difficulty alone cannot provide the guidance pupils need to improve.

7. Explain the model’s decisions

The first body paragraph connects difficulty to motivation through clear expectations. The second develops fairness and adds feedback as a distinct way to improve learning. The conclusion answers the question without claiming that harder exams are always useless.

Underline where each required point is addressed. Then identify one sentence that explains how a benefit could arise. If you cannot find it in your own draft, add the missing reasoning rather than another introductory phrase.

Check your understanding

Try first: For notes “fairness; motivation; your own idea” about exams, choose an additional point and explain its relevance.

Write your attempt before opening a hint. Keep the first version so that you can see what you changed.

I am stuck: show a thinking question

Choose something not already covered by fairness or motivation.

Compare with a possible answer

Feedback after assessment can be a distinct contribution: it helps pupils identify and practise a missing skill. Show how it improves education rather than merely naming it.

Other defensible answers may work. Compare the reasoning and the task requirements, not just the wording.

Apply it independently

Write a fresh 140-190-word practice response using the exact B2 task instructions.
