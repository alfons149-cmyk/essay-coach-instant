1. Plan a judgement, not two disconnected descriptions

Begin with the exact decision the task asks you to make. Select two listed points, decide how they can be compared, and note a provisional priority. You may change that judgement while writing if the developed reasoning supports a different answer.

A plan does not need a sentence for every idea. It needs a clear purpose for each paragraph and enough detail to explain a reason. If both planned paragraphs say only that something is useful, identify what makes their contributions different.

2. Read the points and optional comments accurately

Original practice task: a town wants to improve residents’ access to learning. Points discussed are library study space; free online courses; evening workshops. Comments include: “Not everyone has quiet space.” “Online courses can fit around work.” “Meeting a tutor can make questions easier.”

Write an essay discussing two points. Explain which would do more to improve access to learning in the town, giving reasons. Use your own words as far as possible. Target: 220-260 words.

These are C1-style points and comments, not two C2 source texts. Choose two points and develop the comparison rather than summarising every comment.

3. Define a useful comparison criterion

For access to learning, ask who faces a barrier and how each proposal could reduce it. Price, opening times, internet access and confidence can matter. You cannot decide priority merely by listing more advantages for your favourite option.

Choose library space and online courses. A provisional judgement might favour library space where quiet workspace and reliable internet are the main barriers. State that condition; do not invent a survey claiming those barriers have been measured.

4. Develop each idea with a mechanism and detail

Library: a quiet desk can give someone sharing a crowded home a place to work. If computers are available, the space may also support online study. Online courses: flexible access can help shift workers, but only if they have equipment and a usable connection.

These benefits relate rather than simply oppose each other. A library can supply conditions that make online learning possible. That relationship can support a priority without dismissing the second option.

5. Build a short outline

Introduction: select library space and online courses; identify practical access as the criterion.
Body 1: develop flexibility of online learning and the equipment/space limitation.
Body 2: explain how library facilities address that limitation, while acknowledging opening hours and cost.
Conclusion: prioritise suitable library facilities where basic access is the main obstacle.

Try reversing the body paragraphs. Which order makes the final judgement easier to follow? There can be more than one defensible answer. Name the relationship before choosing a linker.

6. Fair comparison does not mean equal word counts

Develop both selected points enough for a meaningful comparison. You do not have to give each exactly half the words or manufacture an equally strong objection to each.

Test your preferred option against a real constraint. If a library closes before residents finish work, its access benefit may be limited. A sensible conclusion should retain that condition or propose a proportionate response.

7. Move from a plan to a developed paragraph

Online courses could make study easier to fit around irregular working hours. A resident could review a lesson after a late shift instead of attending a fixed class. That flexibility would be less useful, however, without a reliable connection and a suitable place to concentrate.

The ending identifies a limit that the next paragraph can address: Library facilities could help meet those practical requirements... This is evaluation through a relevant condition, not through adding the word nevertheless at random.

8. Review before drafting the complete answer

Check that your plan discusses two listed points, uses a relevant comparison criterion, explains your provisional priority and fits the word budget. Keep a little time for revision rather than writing the whole essay into the plan.

Then write independently. After feedback, try a new task: repeating the same polished plan does not show that you can make these decisions on a fresh question.

Check your understanding

Try first: Compare library space and online courses for improving access to learning. Find a relationship beyond “both are useful”.

Write your attempt before opening a hint. Keep the first version so that you can see what you changed.

I am stuck: show a thinking question

Could one proposal help meet a condition needed by the other?

Compare with a possible answer

Library space with internet access could make online courses usable for residents without suitable facilities. This supports prioritising facilities where that barrier matters, while retaining online learning’s value.

Other defensible answers may work. Compare the reasoning and the task requirements, not just the wording.

Apply it independently

Write a new plan and explain why your preferred option deserves priority for the task’s target group.
